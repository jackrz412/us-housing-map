How the stress index works, and what its backtest showed

The map's Index layers combine several measures of mortgage strain into two scores per county. This page gives the formulas, the data behind them, the results of a test against later delinquency, and the limits of what the scores can tell you.

The index describes current conditions. It does not predict defaults.

A backtest on 2019 data found that the Burden score did not anticipate how county delinquency changed by 2023–2025. That test period also lacked the economic shock that usually turns strain into default, so the result can't settle the question either way. Details below.

1. The two scores

Mortgage defaults usually need two things at once: a household that is stretched, and a shock such as a job loss or a fall in home prices. The index measures the first part only, in two halves:

  • Burden: can owners keep paying? Payment burden for new buyers and existing owners.
  • Equity: could they sell or refinance their way out? How much recent lending was at high loan-to-value.

Every input is divided by a fixed benchmark, so 1.0 means "at the benchmark" and 1.2 means 20% above it:

x_i      = c_i / b_i

NewBuyer = mean(x_PTI, x_D, x_R)
Existing = x_S
Burden   = 0.5 × NewBuyer + 0.5 × Existing

Equity   = x_CLTV

c_i is the county's value and b_i its benchmark. NewBuyer averages whichever of PTI, D and R the county has.

InputWhat it measuresBenchmark

  • Benchmarks are the 2019 national values (50 states and DC), computed once and never recomputed, so a score means the same thing every year. The PTI benchmark is the traditional rule that housing should take at most 28% of gross income.
  • Weights are a stated choice, not fitted: equal within each half, and half-and-half across Burden's two halves. They were not tuned on the backtest and won't be.
  • Burden needs both halves: at least one of PTI, D or R, plus S. Each input needs at least 30 loans, applications or owner households in the county.
  • Equity uses conventional loans only. Most FHA, VA and USDA loans are above 90% CLTV by program design, so including them would mostly map where government lending is common.

2. Payment-to-income, loan by loan

For each home-purchase loan, the monthly payment (principal and interest, property tax, insurance) is divided by monthly income. The county value is the median.

PTI = (M + T + I) / (income / 12)
M   = L · r(1+r)^n / ((1+r)^n − 1)
T   = τ · V / 12
I   = premium / 12
  • L = loan amount; r = annual interest rate ÷ 12; n = the loan's term in months
  • τ = the county's effective property tax rate; V = property value
  • premium = the county's average annual homeowners insurance premium

A loan counts only if it passes every rule, checked in this order:

  1. not interest-only, balloon or negative-amortization (the formula doesn't describe those payments);
  2. interest rate above 0 and at most 20%;
  3. loan amount and property value above zero;
  4. income at least $10,000;
  5. the county has a property tax rate and an insurance premium;
  6. the computed PTI is at most 1.0.

Loans with no reported term use 30 years. In 2025, the rules kept 2,761,544 of 2,976,799 qualifying loans (93%). Counties where the loan-level rules remove more than 10% of loans are flagged in the map's click panel. Often that's because small lenders are exempt from reporting the interest rate.

4. Reading the map

  • Burden score and Equity score: blue below the benchmark, red above, grey for 0.9–1.1 ("near benchmark"). The other breaks are 0.67, 0.8, 1.25 and 1.5. Because benchmarks are fixed at 2019 levels and payments have risen since, most counties now score above 1.0 on Burden.
  • Burden × Equity: each score in three classes (below 0.9, 0.9–1.1, 1.1 and above) on a 3×3 grid. The top-right cell, the darkest, is where both are above benchmark: heavy burden and thin equity. A county needs both scores to appear.
  • Click a county to see both scores and every input, each as its value and its multiple of the benchmark (e.g. "43.8% · 1.53×").
  • Blue means below the benchmark, not "safe", and red means above it, not "about to default".

5. The backtest

The question: did counties that scored higher on 2019 data see delinquency get worse afterwards? The design and every analysis choice were written down before any 2019 county score was computed, and the test was run once.

  • Scores: the same code as the map, run on 2019 inputs.
  • Counties: the 499 counties CFPB publishes, minus Connecticut's 8 planning regions (their 2019 inputs use the old county lines). They're mostly metropolitan.
  • Outcome: each county's average 90+ day delinquency rate in 2023–2025 minus its 2017–2019 average. 2020–2022 is left out, because pandemic forbearance kept missed payments from being reported as delinquent.
  • Measure: Spearman rank correlation (ρ), where positive means "higher score, bigger rise". 95% confidence intervals come from 1,000 bootstrap resamples of counties.

Result: the opposite of the expected direction

Counties with a higher 2019 Burden score saw delinquency fall more. The result was the same using 2018 inputs (ρ = −0.16) and using a 2024–2025 outcome window (ρ = −0.11). Burden did no better than its own inputs: PTI ranked counties closer to the expected direction than Burden did.

Why: regression to the mean

Delinquency fell in 92% of these counties between 2017–2019 and 2023–2025, and how far a county fell depended mostly on where it started (ρ = −0.77 between the starting level and the change). Counties that started high fell the most. Burden, S and low income are all somewhat higher where delinquency started higher, so a change measure pulls them negative whether or not they carry a real signal.

A second test, controlling for the starting level

After seeing that result, and before computing anything new, one further test was specified and recorded. Among counties that started at the same 2017–2019 level, did higher-scoring ones end 2023–2025 higher? This is a partial rank correlation with the 2023–2025 level, controlling for the 2017–2019 level.

Burden shows no relationship. D, R and the Equity score point the expected way, but PTI and S point the other way and cancel them out inside Burden. Median income alone does better than Burden. This test was chosen after the first result was known, so it is suggestive at most, and the weights were not changed because of it.

Why this period can't really test the idea

  • No shock. The index measures strain, and strain usually turns into default only when a shock hits. 2019–2025 mostly lacked one: unemployment was low apart from a brief 2020 spike, CARES Act forbearance let borrowers pause payments without being reported delinquent, and home prices rose sharply, so even owners with thin equity could usually sell or refinance.
  • No crisis-era data. The obvious test would be the 2007–2010 foreclosure crisis, but HMDA only began collecting debt-to-income, combined loan-to-value, interest rate, loan term and property value with 2018 data. Before that, PTI, D, R and CLTV can't be computed.

So the backtest neither shows that the index works nor that it doesn't. What it rules out is presenting the scores as a forecast. They measure real conditions (payments relative to income, debt-to-income lending and denials, cost-burdened owners, high-CLTV lending) against 2019. They don't say where defaults will happen.

6. Limitations

  • Non-mortgage debt enters only through D and R, i.e. only for new buyers. Existing owners' card, auto and student debt isn't measured.
  • Insurance is out of date and incomplete. The map pairs 2025 loans with 2022 premiums, unadjusted, and premiums rose faster than inflation, so payments are likely understated. FIO covers the largest insurers' standard owner policies only. It excludes state insurers of last resort (such as Florida's Citizens) and surplus-lines policies, and it doesn't publish ZIPs with few insurers or policies. 29 of Florida's 67 counties have no premium, so they get no PTI.
  • Timing differs across inputs. PTI, D and R describe this year's buyers; S describes all mortgaged owners over a 5-year survey window.
  • The backtest covers 499 mostly metropolitan counties, skips 2020–2022, and compares recent buyers' conditions with delinquency across all existing mortgages.
  • Weights and benchmarks are choices, not estimates. A score of 1.0 means "at the 2019 national level", not "safe".
